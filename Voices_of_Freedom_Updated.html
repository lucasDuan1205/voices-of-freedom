The project

Listening is an act of remembering.

Spirituals emerged among enslaved African Americans, drawing on biblical stories, African-derived musical practices, and the lived realities of enslavement. These songs could offer spiritual strength, express longing, and build a sense of community. Their meanings have continued to change through performance, remembrance, and struggles for justice.

This interactive project invites you not only to learn about these musical histories but also to reflect on what freedom means in your own life. How can listening lead to empathy, memory, and action?

01 / History

Songs across generations

01 · ORIGINS

Music under slavery

Enslaved African Americans developed spirituals through communal singing, religious expression, and shared experience. Biblical images of liberation and deliverance often resonated with their circumstances.

02 · MEMORY

Preserving voices

After emancipation, performers and institutions such as the Fisk Jubilee Singers helped introduce spirituals to wider audiences, while Black communities continued to shape their meanings and traditions.

03 · ACTION

Freedom songs

During the Civil Rights Movement, activists adapted and drew on spiritual traditions to build solidarity and courage. Songs became tools for collective participation, not simply performances to observe.

What changes when we hear a song not only as art, but also as a voice from history?

Historical note: Spirituals have diverse origins and meanings. Claims that particular songs universally contained coded escape instructions are debated and should not be presented as established fact.

Keep listening

From memory to action

Musical activism is not limited to public protest. It can begin with preserving histories, questioning whose voices are heard, and creating opportunities for participation. The aim of this project is to invite more attentive listening and reflection—not to claim that one website can resolve the struggles represented in these songs.
